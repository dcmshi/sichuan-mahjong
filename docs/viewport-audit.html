Where the board runs off a phone

Twenty-five screen-and-device combinations measured against their own viewport. Ten overflow. The pattern is not evenly spread: portrait phones are close to fitting, landscape phones are not close at all, and one screen is the worst case on every device.

What needs a decision

1

Landscape phone has no layout of its own

Every screen overflows in landscape, and play needs roughly twice the viewport height. The board is built as a vertical stack — opponent across the top, two opponents down the sides, your hand at the bottom — which is the right shape for a 390×664 portrait window and the wrong one for an 844×340 landscape window, where there is width to spare and almost no height.

Right now landscape is playable only by scrolling, which moves your own hand off screen exactly when you need to tap it.

Question for design Is landscape worth supporting? If yes it wants a genuinely different arrangement rather than the portrait stack compressed — the wide viewport could put the three opponents in a row across the top and keep the hand pinned. If no, the honest move is to detect landscape and ask the player to rotate.
2

Round end is the worst screen on every device

It overflows on all five profiles, including the iPad in landscape. It stacks four expandable result rows, then four match-total rows, then two buttons — and a row expands to show that seat's revealed hand, its fan list and an itemised list of payments.

Scrolling a results screen is far more acceptable than scrolling the board, so this may simply be fine. But the winner's row opens by default, which is what pushes the buttons down: on an iPhone SE the two most important controls sit 416px below the fold.

Question for design Should the round-end actions be pinned to the bottom of the viewport rather than sitting at the end of a long scroll? And should the winner's detail still be open on arrival, or collapsed with the scoreline first?
3

Portrait play fits, with nothing to spare

The board now lands within about 5% of an iPhone 14's viewport and about 23% over on the smaller SE. That came from replacing each side opponent's thirteen individual tile backs with a short overlapped stack and a count — the old version was 687px of the total on its own.

The remaining height is real content rather than slack: the top bar, the score strip, the across-opponent zone, your discard tray, and your hand. There is no obvious next reduction that does not cost information.

Question for design The smallest phones still need ~130px. The candidates are a denser discard tray, a shorter across-opponent zone, or folding the score strip into the top bar. Which of those is least costly to the read of the board?

Constraints worth knowing

  • Your own discard tray has to show the full history. Furiten — whether you are allowed to win on a discard — is decided by what you have already discarded, so truncating it removes the information a player needs to reason about their own hand.
  • Tile art is fixed-aspect SVG (210×255). Tiles can scale, but not be reproportioned, and below roughly 24px wide the suit markings stop being readable.
  • Tap targets are 40px minimum across all chrome, set during the accessibility pass. Shrinking controls to buy height would undo that.
  • Opponent hands are public information only as a count. Their concealed tiles are never sent to the client, so any representation is free to be as compact as it likes — it is drawing a number, not tiles.
  • The app installs as a PWA. Installed to a home screen there is no browser chrome, so the viewport is taller than the figures here, which are measured in a browser tab.

How to reproduce

Device profiles are Playwright's, which model a browser tab with its chrome visible — an iPhone 14 reports 390×664 rather than 390×844. Screens were driven through a real practice game; play was sampled on every turn and the peak recorded. Horizontal overflow is already guarded in CI by e2e/ui-clicks.spec.ts, which runs real taps on five viewports.
